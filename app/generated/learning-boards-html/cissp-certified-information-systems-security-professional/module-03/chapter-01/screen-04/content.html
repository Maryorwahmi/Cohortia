Module 3 › Chapter 3.1 › Secure Design Principles — What they are and why they matter
Definition

What a secure design principle is

Principles constrain design to reduce risk, support verification, and guide consistent controls across an architecture.

Concrete example
A web server that only needs to read files should not have write access to system directories; least privilege reduces potential damage if the server is compromised.
Common mistake
Treating principles as optional add-ons rather than constraints leads to inconsistent, weak architectures.
Key Takeaway

Use principles to limit design choices and produce systems that are easier to verify and maintain.

Principles List
Least Privilege
Grant only the permissions necessary. Limits attack surface and contains compromise.
Separation of Duties
Split critical tasks across people or processes to reduce fraud and single-person errors.
Defense in Depth
Multiple independent layers so one failure does not imply total compromise.
Fail-Safe Defaults
When failure occurs, deny access rather than permitting it by default.
Economy of Mechanism
Keep designs simple; complexity hides bugs and increases verification cost.
Complete Mediation
Every access must be checked by the access control mechanism, not only the initial request.
Open Design & Psychological Acceptability
Favor peer-reviewed mechanisms and controls that users can follow easily to avoid risky workarounds.
Why this matters in architecture
  • Principles constrain attackers' paths and improve auditability.
  • Simplicity accelerates verification and reduces hidden risks.
  • Usable controls maintain security over time by avoiding human bypasses.
