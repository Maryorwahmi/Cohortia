Self-Sovereign Identity (SSI)
Individuals manage verifiable credentials and control selective disclosure.
Core definition

SSI gives users cryptographic keys and credentials issued by authorities; users present proofs without exposing full records.

Concrete example

A clinic requests proof of vaccination. The patient provides a signed verifiable credential that reveals only status, not full medical history.

Common mistake

Assuming SSI removes the need for trusted issuers. SSI changes control of disclosure but still relies on trusted signing authorities.

Design considerations
  • Store only hashes or revocation pointers on-chain to protect privacy.
  • Provide user-friendly key recovery that balances security and accessibility.
  • Define clear issuer governance to avoid credential abuse or discriminatory practices.
Key Takeaway

SSI can reduce central breach risk and improve privacy, but success depends on usable recovery and issuer trust models.

Analogy

Think of credentials like sealed envelopes signed by trusted authorities; you choose which envelope to open for a verifier.
