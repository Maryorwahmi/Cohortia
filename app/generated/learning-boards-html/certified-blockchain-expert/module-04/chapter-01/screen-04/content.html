Definition

Permission models and their consequences

Permissioned networks require authorization and identity management; permissionless networks allow open participation. The choice affects compliance, auditability, and access control.

Key consequence: Identity
Known participants mean actions can be traced to legal entities for compliance and dispute resolution.
Key consequence: Access control
Selective visibility prevents sensitive data exposure while preserving a shared record.
Key Takeaway

Permissioning preserves a shared ledger but adds identity, which is essential for regulated multi-party business processes.

Concrete example
Consortium of banks
Banks operating shared settlement infrastructure require participant identity to meet regulatory and audit requirements. Permissioned design provides this while enabling a single source of truth for settlements.
Common misconception
Not a private database
A permissioned ledger is still a shared, append-only record among participants; it differs from a siloed database because it enforces replicated state and shared validation rules.
