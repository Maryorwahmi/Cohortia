Module 5 › Chapter 5.1 › Decentralization
Definition & Importance
Decentralization for resilience
Multiple independent nodes hold and validate state, preventing a single compromised operator from rewriting history unilaterally.
How it increases security
  • Removes single points of failure and privileged control.
  • Makes consensus require broad agreement, raising cost for attackers.
  • Enables cross-checking and independent validation by peers.
Misconception
Decentralization is not binary. A network with many nodes but poor peer diversity or weak identity controls can be effectively centralized in practice.
Practical evaluation checklist
  • Node count and geography: Are nodes distributed across providers and regions?
  • Validator diversity: Are many independent entities running validators, or a few large pools?
  • Peer discovery: Does the protocol resist identity spoofing or rapid node creation?
Analogy
Decentralization is like a city with many independent observers. A single corrupt official can harm a small town but has less influence in a large, diverse city.
Key Takeaway

Decentralization makes attacks harder by spreading power, but it must be measured in effective diversity, not just node numbers.
