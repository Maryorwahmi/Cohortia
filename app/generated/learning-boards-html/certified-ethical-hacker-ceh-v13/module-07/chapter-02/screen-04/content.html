Core Concept › Attack Surfaces for Cryptography

Definition and practical examples

Attack surfaces are the parts of a system where an attacker can apply effort to obtain secrets or bypass protections without needing to cryptanalyze algorithms.

Stored hashes without salt
Enables rainbow-table attacks because identical passwords share identical hashes.
Short passwords or PINs
Make brute-force or exhaustive search practical in reasonable time.
Weak key management
Keys stored with overly broad access or in plaintext permit practical key theft.
Key Takeaway

Focus first on accessible, fixable weaknesses around keys and secrets; they are the most common route attackers take.

Supporting explanation

  • Implementation errors and configurations are exploited more often than mathematical flaws.
  • Check whether salts, iteration counts, and key lengths meet modern guidance; absence suggests targeted tests.
  • Human factors—reused passwords, poor rotation, exposed backups—create low-cost attack paths.
Concrete scenario
You find a database dump with password hashes and no salt column. That artifact points you to precomputed table or dictionary-style attacks as a first, low-impact test.
Common misconception
Believing a strong algorithm alone guarantees safety. In reality poor configuration or key handling often undermines strength.
