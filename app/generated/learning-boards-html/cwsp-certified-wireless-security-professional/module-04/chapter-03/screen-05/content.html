How Deauthentication Floods Work

The deauthentication flood exploits a fundamental flaw in the 802.11 standard: management frames are sent unauthenticated and unencrypted.

Spoofed MAC Addresses

An attacker spoofs the MAC address of either the AP or a client to send fake deauthentication frames.

Unauthenticated Frames

The 802.11 standard allows deauthentication frames to be sent without cryptographic verification.

Continuous Disruption

A continuous flood prevents clients from connecting or maintaining a stable wireless link.

Example: Targeted Client Disconnection

Forcing a User Offline

An attacker identifies a specific client connected to a Wi-Fi network. By spoofing the AP's MAC address and sending deauthentication frames to that client, the attacker can repeatedly force the client to disconnect, making the network unusable for them. This can be used to target specific individuals or to force them onto a rogue AP.

Network Outage Risk
Key Takeaway

Deauthentication floods leverage unauthenticated 802.11 management frames to spoof AP or client MACs, causing forced disconnections and rendering wireless service unusable for targeted devices.
