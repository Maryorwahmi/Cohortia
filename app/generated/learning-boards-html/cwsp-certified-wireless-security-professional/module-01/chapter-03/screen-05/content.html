WEP's Fatal Flaws: IV Reuse and RC4 Weakness

How a small Initialization Vector and static keys led to WEP's downfall

The 24-bit Initialization Vector (IV)

  • Definition: A non-secret number combined with a secret key for encryption.
  • WEP's Problem: 24-bit IV is extremely small (16,777,216 unique values).
  • Exposure: Sent in plaintext with every packet, making it easily observable.

IV Collisions and Key Recovery

  • Frequent Reuse: On busy networks, IVs would repeat rapidly.
  • IV Collision: When the same IV is used with the same WEP key.
  • Attack Vector: Attackers collect packets with repeating IVs to perform statistical analysis.
  • Tools: `Aircrack-ng` could decrypt traffic and recover the WEP key in minutes.
Critical Flaw

WEP's static key combined with the small, plaintext IV allowed for easy key recovery, rendering the encryption useless.

RC4 Stream Cipher Weaknesses

  • Stream Cipher: Encrypts data bit by bit, fast but sensitive to key reuse.
  • Key Scheduling Algorithm (KSA): RC4's KSA had weaknesses that, when combined with IV reuse, made it vulnerable.
  • Related-Key Attacks: Possible to deduce parts of the key stream.

Lack of Integrity Checking

  • WEP used a simple Cyclic Redundancy Check (CRC-32) for integrity.
  • CRC-32 is not cryptographically secure; attackers could easily modify packets and recalculate the correct CRC-32.
  • This allowed for undetected packet manipulation and injection.
Key Takeaway

The combination of a small, plaintext IV, static keys, and weak integrity checks made WEP fundamentally insecure and easily exploitable.
