Module 2 › Chapter 2.1 › What DRY Really Means
Definition
Every piece of knowledge has one authoritative representation
This includes logic, data models, configuration, and business rules. DRY reduces places a change must be made and avoids inconsistent behavior.
Concrete example
Email validation repeated in three handlers; centralize to a single is_valid_email function used everywhere.
Common misconception
Not every duplicate line is DRY-violation. Ask whether a change in intent requires changes across copies.
Key Takeaway

DRY protects knowledge, not trivial repetition. Centralize business rules and data definitions to keep behavior consistent.

Analogy
Imagine legal terms stored as copies in multiple contract templates. If law changes, you must update every template. Instead, reference a single clause so updates are reliable.
Check your understanding
  • Is a repeated log message a DRY problem? Only if the log encodes critical business meaning.
  • Does a single change require edits across files? If yes, centralize it.
Practical tip
When you identify duplication, write tests first for the behavior. Then extract the logic to ensure behavior remains unchanged after the refactor.
