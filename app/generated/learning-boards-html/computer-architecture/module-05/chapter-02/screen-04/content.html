Why fetching multiple instructions per cycle is hard

Understand the three reasons a fetch pipeline cannot always deliver steady packs of instructions.

Detailed points
  • Cache line boundaries: An instruction may span lines, requiring two L1I accesses and alignment logic before decode.
  • Taken branches: Redirects create bubbles; even a correct prediction often wastes a cycle while the fetch stream re-targets.
  • Variable-length ISAs: Without predecoding, the fetcher can’t know where one instruction ends and the next begins, making parallel fetch tricky.
  • Combined effect: These issues force multi-line fetch, speculative fetch, and buffering rather than a single fast read per cycle.
Concrete example
A 64-byte line holds 16 fixed 4-byte instructions. If the fifth instruction starts at byte 60, bytes for that instruction live across two lines and two reads are needed to fetch a continuous stream of decoded instructions.
Common misconception
Thinking in bytes per cycle alone ignores ordering and boundaries; bytes are necessary but not sufficient for several-instruction delivery when instructions cross boundaries.
Analogy & takeaway
Imagine a delivery truck that can carry four packages. If a package is half in one warehouse and half in another, the truck must visit both warehouses before delivering the package — the trip becomes less efficient. Likewise, taken-branch detours waste a delivery trip. The solution is to add sorting workers (predecoder) and a loading dock (instruction queue) so the factory keeps working while the truck resolves its route.
Key Takeaway

Fetch bandwidth depends on instruction layout and control flow; caches, predictors, and buffers are co-designed to hide boundaries and redirects.
